Knowing Grammar and Being Able to Speak Are Two Different Things

You can conjugate correctly on paper, remember every gender agreement rule, and still freeze when asked to answer a question out loud. That's not a sign you're "bad at grammar" — it's evidence you're missing a completely different skill: speaking reflex.

Hai Bui · DALF C2, 50/50 on Speaking · Oui Academy

Two different systems in the brain

Linguistics draws a clear line between two kinds of ability: explicit knowledge (memorized rules you can state and explain) and implicit competence (automatic language use, no conscious rule-checking involved). Studying grammar from a book builds the first extremely well. But fluent speaking requires the second — and one doesn't automatically convert into the other just by studying more.

Why "study more grammar" doesn't fix the speaking problem

When people notice their speaking is weak, the instinct is often to... study more grammar. It sounds logical — but in practice, someone who's already reached B1-B2 grammar knowledge usually isn't missing knowledge; they're missing the hours of practice that convert that knowledge into a real-time reflex. Learning new rules while the reflex for existing ones hasn't formed just grows the pile of "knowledge I can't use yet" without improving speaking.

Explicit knowledge

  • Learned from books, rules, grammar exercises
  • Can explain the rule back
  • Needs thinking time to apply
  • Measured by written tests

Implicit competence (reflex)

  • Built through repeated speaking practice with feedback
  • Used automatically, no rule recall needed
  • Appears instantly while speaking
  • Only measurable through real speaking practice
Why this matters for exam prep TCF/TEF examiners grade the Speaking section on real-time natural speech, not on the ability to explain a grammar rule. Learners with solid grammar on paper but no built-up reflex routinely score noticeably lower on Speaking than on Reading — a clear sign of the gap between the two kinds of ability.

Building reflex, not just knowledge

Reflex only forms through repeated practice with immediate feedback — not through reading more grammar. This is exactly why the shadowing method works: it forces the brain to process and produce language in real time, which is exactly the reflex the Speaking section requires.

That doesn't mean grammar doesn't matter — a solid grammar foundation is still needed to speak correctly. But once you have that foundation (usually around A2-B1), the next step isn't more grammar study — it's converting that knowledge into reflex through real speaking practice.
