Learning French for Canadian Immigration: A Complete Guide to TCF/TEF and the Method That Works

Knowing French is one of the few advantages you can build for an Express Entry application on your own terms — it doesn't depend on luck or your occupation. But "learning French" and "learning it the right way to hit NCLC 7" are two different things. Here's everything you need: why French matters, whether to sit TCF or TEF, and the training method that actually moves the needle on each skill.

Hai Bui · DALF C2, 50/50 on Speaking · Oui Academy

Why French is such a strong lever for Express Entry

Since 2023, IRCC has run category-based draws alongside general Express Entry draws, and French proficiency is one of the standing priority categories — meaning candidates with strong enough French have a real shot at being invited in rounds with a much lower CRS cutoff than general draws.

On top of that, hitting NCLC 7 or higher across all 4 French skills adds direct bonus points to your CRS (Comprehensive Ranking System) score — an even bigger bonus if your English is also strong. Between the two, plenty of applications that were stuck on English scores or work experience have found a path forward through French.

In other words: English ability and work experience are largely things you already have, and are slow to change. French is the rare variable you can actively train in a few months and meaningfully shift your outcome.

TCF Canada or TEF Canada?

Both exams are recognized by IRCC for Express Entry and convert to the same NCLC scale — they mainly differ in who administers them (France Éducation international for TCF, Pearson/Alliance Française for TEF) and the exam format. Neither is objectively "easier"; the right pick depends on which format plays to your strengths.

If you haven't decided yet, the detailed TCF vs. TEF Canada comparison on this blog breaks down each section so you choose correctly the first time — picking the wrong exam and having to re-learn the format is the single most common way learners waste time.

The NCLC scale: how far do you actually need to train?

TCF and TEF Canada both score 4 skills independently, then convert each to the NCLC 1–12 scale. NCLC 7 is generally treated as the "strong French" threshold for immigration purposes, but here's the part that trips people up: NCLC is scored per skill — you might be NCLC 8 on Listening/Reading but only NCLC 5 on Speaking, and it's your weakest skill that dominates how much your application actually gains.

The post TCF Canada: How Many Points Do You Need for Immigration? breaks down the conversion in detail and why "passing" isn't the right target — you need NCLC 7 specifically on your weakest skill, which is usually Speaking.

Important note CRS points, priority NCLC thresholds, and the category-based draw calendar change with every IRCC round. The information above is directional — always check IRCC's official CRS calculator and the latest announcements before planning your immigration timeline.

Why the traditional way of studying usually isn't enough

Most self-learners and general-purpose courses spend most of their time on grammar and vocabulary — effective for the multiple-choice Listening/Reading sections, but it barely moves Speaking or Writing, which examiners grade on your ability to reason and respond in real time, not on whether you can spot the right answer on paper.

This is the single most common reason an application ends up with lopsided NCLC scores: you trained to read a French newspaper, but never once practiced answering an examiner's question in 30 seconds.

A more effective method: shadowing — listen, repeat, get scored

Shadowing (listening and repeating almost simultaneously with a native speaker) is a speaking-reflex technique widely used in interpreter training and communication-focused language programs. Unlike passive grammar study, shadowing forces your brain to process intonation, pace, and sentence structure while you're producing speech — exactly the reflex TCF/TEF examiners are grading.

What makes it work far better than doing it alone is immediate feedback: without it, learners usually can't tell which mistakes they're repeating, and repeated mistakes get harder to unlearn the longer they go uncorrected. Pairing shadowing with a score or note right after each attempt — whether automated or from a teacher — noticeably shortens the gap between "can speak" and "speaks correctly."

1

Listen to a short clip (10–20 seconds) on a topic that actually shows up in the TCF/TEF Speaking section — description, storytelling, argument.

2

Repeat it immediately, matching the original intonation as closely as you can — don't translate in your head first; the target is reflex, not translation.

3

Get scored or corrected right after — know exactly which sound or pace was off, instead of guessing.

4

Repeat the corrected version — drill the exact spot you got wrong before moving on, so it doesn't resurface on exam day.

An effective prep plan usually combines: daily shadowing (15–20 minutes) to build reflex, a weekly Speaking/Writing session graded against real exam criteria, and timed mock exams as test day gets closer.

Checklist before you register

  • You've set a clear target — NCLC 7 on your weakest skill, not an average score.
  • You've picked TCF or TEF based on which format suits you, not just the nearest test date.
  • You've practiced under real time pressure at least a few times before test day — not just drilled sections in isolation.
  • You've checked the latest CRS calculator and priority thresholds on IRCC's official site before finalizing your application timeline.

French isn't a barrier here — it's the rare part of your Express Entry application you can actually control. Train the right skills the right way, and your application gets stronger faster than you'd expect.
